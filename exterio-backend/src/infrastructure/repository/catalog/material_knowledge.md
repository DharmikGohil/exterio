# Material knowledge base

Short reference notes used by the material advisor. Each `##` section is one retrievable note.
These are general guidance for low-rise homes in India, not product specifications.

## Exterior emulsion paint: where it works and how long it lasts

Acrylic exterior emulsion is the lowest-cost finish and suits any plastered wall, parapet,
pillar or balcony face. Expect 5 to 7 years before repainting for a standard weatherproof
grade and 8 to 10 years for premium or elastomeric grades. It needs a sound, dry, primed
surface: paint will not hide cracks or damp, it will peel over them. Light colours reflect
heat and fade less; dark colours on sun-facing walls fade faster and show dust. Budget one
coat of primer and two coats of paint. Typical coverage is about 55 sq ft per litre for two
coats on a smooth wall, less on rough plaster.

## Elastomeric and premium paints: when the extra cost is worth it

Elastomeric coatings stretch, so they bridge hairline cracks that would reopen under ordinary
emulsion. Choose them for walls with fine cracking, for parapets and terraces that take direct
rain, and for coastal or high-rainfall areas. They cost roughly one and a half times a
standard emulsion but usually add three or more years to the repaint cycle, so the cost per
year is often lower.

## Texture finish: hiding uneven plaster

Trowel or roller applied texture coats are thicker than paint, so they hide undulating
plaster and minor cracks. They suit feature walls, parapets and boundary walls. The rough
surface holds dust and is harder to clean, so on a busy road pick a finer grain and plan a
pressure wash once a year. Texture is harder to patch invisibly than flat paint, so keep some
material from the same batch for repairs. Lasts 8 to 10 years.

## Natural stone cladding: where to use it and what to check first

Stone cladding (sandstone, slate, stacked ledge stone) gives the most durable and premium
look and lasts 25 years or more. It is heavy and expensive, so it is normally used as an
accent: the entrance wall, pillars, the plinth band or one feature panel, not the whole
house. Check that the wall is structurally sound and the plaster is not hollow before
fixing. Above about 10 ft, stone should be fixed with mechanical clamps as well as adhesive.
Seal natural stone every 2 to 3 years to stop water staining and algae. Avoid porous stone on
walls that stay damp.

## Brick tile cladding: the warm look at lower weight

Thin brick tiles give an exposed-brick look at a fraction of the weight and cost of stone.
They suit feature walls, pillars and compound walls and last 20 years or more with little
maintenance. Use a flexible exterior tile adhesive and leave recessed joints. Re-point
joints if they crack, because water behind the tiles is what makes them fall.

## Elevation tiles: durable and easy to clean

Vitrified or ceramic elevation tiles are factory finished, colourfast and easy to wash, and
last 15 to 20 years. They suit front walls, pillars and balcony faces. The risks are poor
fixing and hollow tiles: insist on exterior-grade adhesive rather than cement slurry, full
back-buttering, and expansion joints on large walls. Dark tiles on west-facing walls get very
hot and show water marks. Order 10 percent extra from one batch, because shades differ
between batches.

## Wood-look tiles and HPL boards compared with real wood

Real timber outdoors needs polishing every year or two and suffers in monsoon humidity.
Wood-look vitrified planks need no maintenance and do not fade. Exterior-grade HPL boards
look closer to real wood and are lighter, but cost several times more and can fade on walls
that take strong afternoon sun. For a wood accent on a budget choose wood-look tiles; choose
HPL when a seamless board look matters and the wall is shaded.

## ACP panels: modern look, needs a frame

Aluminium composite panels give a flat, modern, metallic look and are common on commercial
fronts. They are fixed on an aluminium sub-frame, not directly on the wall, so they add 2 to
3 inches of thickness and need careful sealing at joints. They last 15 to 20 years but dent
on impact, and sealant joints should be checked every 3 to 4 years. Use fire-retardant grade
panels. They are rarely the best value for a whole bungalow; use them for a canopy fascia or
a feature band.

## Glass railing: open views, more cleaning

A 12 mm toughened glass railing keeps the view open and makes a balcony look larger. It
needs cleaning roughly monthly, shows water spots, and costs about twice a stainless steel
railing. Use toughened or laminated safety glass only, with SS 304 hardware (SS 316 near the
coast). Check that the balcony edge can take the fixing: spigots and channels need sound
concrete, not just tile and screed.

## Stainless steel and mild steel railings compared

Stainless steel 304 railings resist rust, need only wiping, and last 20 years or more. Mild
steel with powder coating or enamel paint costs about half as much but needs repainting every
4 to 5 years, sooner where the coating is scratched. Near the sea use SS 316, because 304
develops tea-staining. For safety, keep baluster gaps under 4 inches and railing height at
least 3.5 ft on upper floors.

## Hot, dry and monsoon climates: choosing finishes for Gujarat and similar regions

Summer heat and strong sun fade dark paints and overheat dark tiles, so prefer light or
mid-tone colours on south and west walls. The monsoon brings driving rain: seal parapet tops,
use a water-repellent or elastomeric coat on exposed walls, and make sure stone and tile
joints are sealed. Dust is the main maintenance issue between monsoons, so smooth or fine
textures stay cleaner than coarse ones.

## Which material goes where on a house front

Main walls: paint for value, texture for uneven plaster, tile or stone only on feature
areas. Pillars and the entrance: stone or brick cladding gives the most visual impact for
the least area. Parapets: paint or texture with a sealed coping, since this surface takes the
most rain. Balcony faces: paint, texture or tile. Railings: glass for views, stainless steel
for low maintenance, mild steel for budget. Gates and grilles: enamel or PU paint over a
rust-preventive primer.

## Getting the most from a limited budget

Spend on the surfaces people see first: the entrance wall, pillars and gate. A common
approach is good-quality paint on all walls plus stone or tile on 10 to 20 percent of the
front as an accent. Cladding a whole wall in stone can cost ten times painting it. Good
surface preparation (crack filling, primer) matters more for how long paint lasts than the
price of the paint itself. Labour is charged on area, so rates for small jobs are often
higher than the per square foot figures suggest.

## Reading and questioning a contractor quotation

Ask for area measurements per surface, not a lump sum, and check them against your own
figures. Confirm whether the rate includes material and labour or labour only, and whether
putty, primer, scaffolding, crack repair and GST are included. Wastage of 5 percent for
paint, about 10 percent for tiles and 10 to 15 percent for stone is normal; much more than
that deserves a question. Ask which brand and grade is quoted and how many coats.
